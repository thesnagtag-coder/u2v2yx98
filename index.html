Before you start - safety first

HSE
Water near electrics: do not touch sockets, switches or light fittings that are wet or stained. A qualified electrician must inspect wet electrical equipment before power is restored. (CDC Electrical)
Sewage or contaminated water: damage caused by sewage or other contaminated water needs a professional experienced with contaminated water. (EPA Mold Cleanup)
  • Water heaters: look only. Do not touch the temperature and pressure relief valve, pipes or controls, and do not open any covers. (Professional practice)
  • Pre-Purchase: ask before running taps, flushing toilets, opening cupboards or reading the meter. (Professional practice)
  • Meter boxes can be in roads or pavements: watch for traffic, and do not lift heavy or damaged covers. (Professional practice)
  • Do not open boxed-in pipes, remove panels or lift flooring. Record and ask. (Professional practice)

Water Meter Test

SHEET 04 · US EPA WATERSENSE

US EPA: check your water meter before and after a two-hour period when no water is being used. If the meter changes at all, you probably have a leak. Make sure nobody uses water, and that appliances (washing machine, dishwasher, irrigation, ice maker) are not running.

Pre-Purchase: ask the owner's permission first. If there is no meter or no access, record that instead.

Toilet Dye Test

US EPA WATERSENSE

US EPA: place a few drops of food colouring in the toilet tank. If any colour shows up in the bowl after 10 minutes, you have a leak. Do not flush during the test. Flush afterwards.

Reference figures (US EPA WaterSense): a tap dripping once per second can waste more than 11,356 L (3,000 US gal) a year; a showerhead leaking 10 drips per minute more than 1,893 L (500 US gal) a year; the average household's leaks can waste more than 35,200 L (9,300 US gal) a year. A family of four using more than 45,400 L (12,000 US gal) a month in winter may have a serious leak. These are US figures for context, not targets.

    How priorities are defined

    SHEET 06 · KEY
    HighAn active leak, a failed meter test, water damage to ceilings, walls or floors, a loose or leaking toilet, a leaking water heater, or water near electrics. Arrange a licensed plumber soon.
    MediumA slow leak (drips, running toilet), slow drains, smells, missing or hard-to-reach shut-off valve, or conditions that need monitoring.
    LowRecorded for completeness, with no current defect seen.

    Priority organises your findings. It is not a hazard rating or a diagnosis. Priority definitions: professional practice.

    What this tool will not do

    LIMITS
    • It will not find the exact location of a hidden leak. A meter test shows that water is being lost, not where.
    • It never asks you to open, dismantle, adjust or repair plumbing, water heaters or valves.
    • It does not check water quality, water heater temperatures or gas appliances, and does not replace a licensed plumber.

    Standards & Measurements

    REFERENCES

    Universal guidance used in this tool

    • Meter test: check the meter before and after a two-hour period with no water use; any change probably means a leak. (EPA WaterSense)
    • Toilet dye test: a few drops of food colouring in the tank; colour in the bowl after 10 minutes means a leak. (EPA WaterSense)
    • What a home inspector checks: the main water shut-off valve (and describes its location), water heating equipment including TPR valves, fixtures and faucets by running water, toilets by flushing, drainage of sinks, tubs and showers, and the drain, waste and vent system. Reports active plumbing leaks, toilet damage, loose floor connections and leaks. (InterNACHI SOP 3.6)
    • Fix leaks fast: fix plumbing leaks and other water problems as soon as possible; dry wet materials within 24-48 hours. Cover cold water pipes with insulation to reduce condensation. (EPA Brief Guide)
    • Moisture sources: leaking or condensing water pipes. Hidden mould can grow in pipe chases. (EPA Mold Course, EPA Remediation Guide)
    • Contaminated water: sewage or contaminated water damage needs an experienced professional. (EPA Mold Cleanup)
    • Wet electrics: a qualified electrician must inspect before power is restored. (CDC Electrical)
    • Units: 1 m³ = 1,000 L; 1 US gallon = 3.785 L. Figures are shown metric first, US gallons in brackets.
    Country-specific rules: plumbing codes, water heater rules, who owns the pipe between the street and the house, backflow requirements and seller disclosure duties differ between countries. EPA figures are US figures for context. Verify local requirements with your water supplier, local authority or a licensed plumber.

    Items marked Professional practice reflect general inspection and HSE practice. No single public standard covers them, so no standard is claimed.
